# defined on Main may be used. The entries come in three parts: the idea in plain words, the ten steps, and every term of every tab.
## In plain words: what this workbook answers
Value at risk (VaR) answers one question: "on a bad day, how much could this portfolio lose?" It does not guess the future. It takes the real market moves of many past days (the scenarios), applies each of them to today's positions, and asks what each position would have gained or lost if that day's move happened again now. Line the results up from the worst loss to the best gain, and VaR is the loss you would exceed only on the few worst days: at 99% confidence, the loss beaten on only 1 day in 100. This workbook does every one of those steps in plain Excel formulas, so you can follow each number back to its inputs, change an input and watch the result move. Nothing is hidden: the same figures the engine reports are recomputed here and compared with the engine to the cent (Main, Checks).
## In plain words: how to read the tabs
Main is the summary: the inputs you may change (yellow cells), the figures derived from them, the results (VaR, expected shortfall, the 10-day figures, each position's share) and the checks against the engine. Config lists each position with its currency, today's value and where its data came from. Data holds each position's value or P&L under every scenario, as delivered. FX holds the exchange rates of every scenario. PnL turns all of that into profit and loss per position and per scenario, adds it up for the portfolio and ranks the days. Explain (this tab) says what every step and every term means. Provenance records where every input came from and when the workbook was made. Read Main first; when a figure surprises you, follow its formula back through PnL to Data and FX.
## In plain words: signs and units
A loss is a negative P&L and a gain a positive one. VaR and expected shortfall are reported as positive numbers: a VaR of 1,000,000 means a loss of 1,000,000 on the bad day. All money is in the reporting currency ({ReportingCurrency:v}) unless a column says otherwise; positions in other currencies are converted through US dollars at each scenario's own rate, so currency moves are part of the risk.
## 1. Each position's P&L under each scenario, in USD
In plain words: for every past day (scenario), how much would this position gain or lose today if that day's market move happened again? Input form PV (the default): P&L(s) = PV(s) × FX_c(s) − PV(base) × FX_c(base), where PV(s) is the position's value under scenario s in its own currency c (Data), FX_c(s) that scenario's exchange rate in USD per unit of c (FX), and PV(base), FX_c(base) today's value and rate (Config). For a USD position this is simply PV(s) − PV(base). Input form PnL: the P&L of each scenario was delivered ready-made (Data); it is converted at each scenario's rate only when it is not in USD. First cell: {PnLFirst}.
## 2. Into the reporting currency ({ReportingCurrency:v})
In plain words: everything is restated in one currency so it can be added up. With p the position's value today in USD (Config, Value today USD) and FX_r the reporting currency's rates (USD per unit): P&L_r(s) = (p + P&L_USD(s)) / FX_r(s) − p / FX_r(base). Every conversion goes through USD, so a EUR position reported in CAD is converted EUR → USD → CAD at each scenario's own rates; in USD reporting this step changes nothing. The Rest of scope column is already in the reporting currency.
## 3. The portfolio's P&L
In plain words: add up every position's gain or loss for the same day. v(s) = the sum across the positions and Rest of scope for scenario s ({PortfolioFirst} for the first scenario). Positions offset each other here: a loss on one and a gain on another on the same day partly cancel, which is why a portfolio's VaR is usually smaller than the sum of its positions' own VaRs (see Diversification).
## 4. Ranked worst first
In plain words: line the days up from the worst loss to the best gain. Rank ({RankFirst}) counts the scenarios with a lower P&L, plus the earlier scenarios with exactly the same P&L, plus one; so the worst day has rank 1, and a tie goes to the older scenario first. Ties are rare, but the rule makes the ranking, and so VaR's day, the same every time.
## 5. VaR
In plain words: VaR is the loss on the k-th worst day, where k is set by the confidence. With {ScenarioCount:v} scenarios and a confidence of {Confidence:v}, k = ⌈N × (1 − Confidence)⌉ = {VarRank} ({VarRank:v}): the k-th worst day. VaR = −(the k-th worst portfolio P&L) = {VaR1} ({VaR1:v}). Read it as: on all but the worst {VarRank:v} of {ScenarioCount:v} days, the loss would have been smaller than this. With RankRule interpolated, the two days around the exact fractional rank are weighted instead (see IPos, ILo, IHi, IW).
## 6. Expected shortfall
In plain words: VaR says where the bad tail starts; expected shortfall (ES) says how bad it is on average once you are in it. k_ES = ⌈N × (1 − EsConfidence)⌉ = {EsRank} ({EsRank:v}); ES = −(the average of the k_ES worst portfolio P&Ls) = {ES1} ({ES1:v}). ES is never smaller than VaR at the same confidence, and regulators favour it because it looks at the whole tail, not one day. On PnL the tail scenarios are marked E and the VaR scenario V.
## 7. Component VaR and ES
In plain words: how much of the portfolio's VaR each position is responsible for; the shares add up to the total exactly. A single day can be noisy, so the components are averaged over a small window of days around VaR's day: ranks k − h to k + h with h = ⌊ComponentWindow / 2⌋ ({VarLo:v} to {VarHi:v}). S = −(the portfolio's mean P&L over the window) = {Smoothed}; the scale c = VaR / S = {Scale} makes the shares add up to VaR itself (ScaleToVar = 1). Component VaR of a position = −c × its mean P&L over the window; component ES = −its mean P&L over the ES tail. A negative component means the position reduces the portfolio's risk (a hedge). The sums are checked on Main.
## 8. Standalone and incremental VaR, diversification
In plain words: standalone VaR is a position's risk on its own, as if nothing else were held; incremental VaR is how much the portfolio's VaR would fall if that position were removed. Standalone VaR and ES apply the same rules as steps 5 and 6 to the position's own column. Incremental VaR (the largest positions by component VaR) = VaR − VaR of the portfolio without the position, from the helper columns at the right of PnL. Diversification = the sum of the standalone VaRs − the portfolio's VaR: the risk that cancels out because positions do not all lose on the same days.
## 9. 10-day
In plain words: regulators also ask for the loss over ten business days. Two methods are shown. √10: the 1-day figure × √10 (about 3.16): VaR × √10 = {VaR10Sqrt} ({VaR10Sqrt:v}); simple, and exact only if days are independent and alike. The sum: each 10-day window is the sum of 10 consecutive scenario P&Ls (10-day sum on PnL, N − 9 windows), ranked and read exactly as steps 4 to 7: {VaR10Sum} ({VaR10Sum:v}). The sum keeps real runs of bad days together, so it can be larger or smaller than √10. Horizon and TenDayMethod say which one is the headline.
## 10. Every confidence and the checks
In plain words: the same ranked days answer every confidence at once, and every answer is checked against the engine. Each configured confidence is a different rank of the same ranked vector (Main, Every confidence). Each result stands beside the engine's own figure with a check; the band at the top of Main says ALL CHECKS PASS when the sheet equals the engine to the cent and the components add up. Change an input and the engine checks turn red (the engine's figure is for the original inputs) while the add-up checks stay green: that is expected, and it is how you explore "what if".
## Term (Main, input): Confidence
The VaR confidence level, for example 0.99 for 99%. It sets how far into the bad days VaR looks: at 0.99, VaR is the loss beaten on only 1% of days; at 0.975, on 2.5%. Higher confidence means a larger VaR. Change it in {Confidence} and every figure that depends on it recalculates; the engine checks then turn red because the engine's figure was for the original confidence. Today: {Confidence:v}.
## Term (Main, input): EsConfidence
The confidence for expected shortfall, often 0.975 (the Basel FRTB standard). ES averages the losses beyond this level. Kept separate from Confidence because rules often ask for VaR at 99% and ES at 97.5%. Cell {EsConfidence}, today {EsConfidence:v}.
## Term (Main, input): Horizon
1D or 10D: whether the headline figures are for one day or ten business days. It does not change the calculation of either; it chooses which one the summary leads with. Cell {Horizon}.
## Term (Main, input): TenDayMethod
How the headline 10-day figure is made: SQRT10 (the 1-day figure × √10) or SUM (VaR read from real 10-day sums of consecutive scenarios). Both are always computed (step 9); this chooses the headline. Cell {TenDayMethod}.
## Term (Main, input): RankRule
How VaR picks its day. kth-worst: exactly the k-th worst day, k rounded up (the regulatory convention and the default). interpolated: when N × (1 − Confidence) is not a whole number, VaR is weighted between the two neighbouring days (see IPos, ILo, IHi, IW); smoother as the confidence changes, but not what most regulators use. Cell {RankRule}.
## Term (Main, input): ComponentWindow
How many days around VaR's day the components average over (step 7), for example 5 means two days either side. A wider window gives steadier, less noisy shares; a window of 1 uses VaR's day alone. It changes how the total is split between positions, never the total. Cell {ComponentWindow}.
## Term (Main, input): ScaleToVar
1 (the default): the components are scaled so they add up exactly to VaR. 0: they add up to the smoothed VaR S of the window instead, unscaled. Use 1 for reports, where the parts must equal the whole. Cell {ScaleToVar}.
## Term (Main, input): ReportingCurrency
The currency every result is reported in, for example USD, CAD or EUR. Each position is converted from its own currency through US dollars at each scenario's own exchange rate (step 2), so exchange-rate moves become part of the risk. Cell {ReportingCurrency}.
## Term (Main, derived): ScenarioCount
N, the number of scenarios (past days) the VaR uses, counted from the portfolio's P&L column; often about 500, two years of business days. More scenarios give a steadier VaR. Cell {ScenarioCount}, today {ScenarioCount:v}.
## Term (Main, derived): VarRank
k, the rank of VaR's day: ⌈N × (1 − Confidence)⌉. With 500 scenarios at 99%, k = 5: VaR is the 5th worst day. Cell {VarRank}, today {VarRank:v}.
## Term (Main, derived): EsRank
k_ES, how many of the worst days ES averages: ⌈N × (1 − EsConfidence)⌉. Cell {EsRank}, today {EsRank:v}.
## Term (Main, derived): VarLo and VarHi
The first and last ranks of the component window around VaR's day (step 7): k − h and k + h, kept within 1 and N. Cells {VarLo} and {VarHi}.
## Term (Main, derived): IPos, ILo, IHi, IW
Used only with RankRule interpolated. IPos = N × (1 − Confidence), the exact rank as a fraction (for example 4.6); ILo and IHi are the whole ranks either side (4 and 5); IW is the weight of the upper one (0.6). VaR is then −(the ILo-th worst × (1 − IW) + the IHi-th worst × IW). Cells {IPos}, {ILo}, {IHi}, {IW}.
## Term (Main, result): VaROneDay
The 1-day VaR (step 5): the loss on the k-th worst day, as a positive number. The figure most people mean by "VaR". Cell {VaROneDay}, today {VaROneDay:v}.
## Term (Main, result): ESOneDay
The 1-day expected shortfall (step 6): the average loss over the k_ES worst days. Cell {ESOneDay}, today {ESOneDay:v}.
## Term (Main, derived): Smoothed
S, the smoothed VaR: −(the portfolio's average P&L over the component window). Close to VaR but less sensitive to one single day; the components are built from it (step 7). Cell {Smoothed}.
## Term (Main, derived): Scale
c = VaR / S when ScaleToVar is 1 (else 1): the factor that makes the components add up exactly to VaR. Close to 1 when VaR's day is typical of its neighbours. Cell {Scale}.
## Term (Main, derived): TenCount
The number of 10-day windows: N − 9, because each window needs ten consecutive scenarios. Cell {TenCount}.
## Term (Main, derived): TenRank, TenEsRank, TenLo, TenHi, TenPos, TenILo, TenIHi, TenIW
The same as VarRank, EsRank, VarLo, VarHi, IPos, ILo, IHi and IW, but counted over the 10-day windows instead of single days (step 9). Cells {TenRank}, {TenEsRank}, {TenLo}, {TenHi}.
## Term (Main, result): VaRTenSum and ESTenSum
The 10-day VaR and ES by the sum method: read from the ranked 10-day sums exactly as the 1-day figures are read from single days. Cells {VaRTenSum} and {ESTenSum}.
## Term (Main, derived): TenSmoothed and TenScale
The smoothed VaR and the scale of the 10-day components, as Smoothed and Scale for single days. Cells {TenSmoothed} and {TenScale}.
## Term (Main, results): VaR 10-day √10 and ES 10-day √10
The 1-day figure × √10 (about 3.162). The classic shortcut from the Basel rules; it assumes days are independent and alike.
## Term (Main, results): Component VaR, Component ES, Component VaR 10-day sum
Each position's share of the portfolio's VaR, ES or 10-day VaR (step 7). They add up to the total (checked). A large positive share marks a main risk driver; a negative one, a hedge.
## Term (Main, results): Standalone VaR and Standalone ES
A position's VaR or ES as if it were the only thing held. Always at least as large as its contribution inside a diversified portfolio, usually larger.
## Term (Main, results): Incremental VaR
How much the portfolio's VaR would fall if the position were removed completely (step 8). Shown for the largest positions by component VaR. Unlike component VaR, incremental VaRs do not add up to the total.
## Term (Main, results): Diversification (Σ standalone − VaR)
The sum of the positions' standalone VaRs minus the portfolio's VaR: the risk that cancels because positions do not all lose on the same days. Larger means a better-balanced portfolio.
## Term (Main, results): VaR scenario (index, rank k), VaR window, ES tail
Which scenario is VaR's day (its index in the scenario set and its rank k), which ranks the component window spans, and which days make up the ES tail. Look these days up on PnL (marked V and E) to see which positions drove the loss.
## Term (Main, results): Smoothed VaR (S), S over the 10-day window
The smoothed VaR of the window for 1 day and for the 10-day sums; the base the components are scaled from.
## Term (Main): Engine, Engine 1-day, Engine √10, Engine sum, Engine component VaR, Engine component ES, Engine incremental VaR
The figure Drishti's VaR engine itself reported for the same scope, date and settings, copied in so the workbook can be compared with it. The workbook never uses them in its own calculation.
## Term (Main): Check, Checks, CHECK FAILED, ALL CHECKS PASS
Each check compares a figure the workbook computed with the engine's figure (equal to the cent) or confirms that parts add up to a whole. ALL CHECKS PASS at the top means every one holds. After you change an input, the comparisons with the engine fail by design (the engine answered the original question); the add-up checks must still pass.
## Term (Main): Every confidence, Every ES confidence
VaR and ES at each confidence the server is configured for (for example 95%, 97.5%, 99%), all read from the same ranked days: a quick view of how risk grows deeper into the tail.
## Term (Config): Position
The position's identifier as the engine knows it. Rest of scope is one extra column standing for every position of the scope not shown individually (when the scope has more than the workbook shows); its P&L is the engine's, already added up, so totals stay exact.
## Term (Config): Kind
The type of the position (for example an interest-rate swap, a bond, an FX forward), as delivered. For Rest of scope, how many positions it stands for.
## Term (Config): Currency
The currency the position's values are in. Its exchange rates are found on FX through the FX column.
## Term (Config): Base PV (own currency)
Today's present value of the position in its own currency: what it is worth now, before any scenario. Scenario P&L is measured against it (step 1).
## Term (Config): Base FX (USD per unit)
Today's exchange rate of the position's currency in US dollars per unit (1 for USD). With Base PV it gives today's value in USD.
## Term (Config): Input form
How the data was delivered: PV (a value under each scenario, from which P&L is computed) or PnL (the P&L under each scenario, delivered ready-made). Both lead to the same P&L (step 1).
## Term (Config): Source
Where the position's data came from: the dataset, table and file the engine read. Provenance has the details.
## Term (Config): Value today (USD)
Base PV × Base FX: today's value in US dollars, the starting point of the currency conversion in step 2.
## Term (Config): FX column
Which column of FX holds this position's currency: the formulas use it to pick the right exchange rate for each scenario.
## Term (Data)
Each position's values (input form PV) or P&L (input form PnL) under every scenario, exactly as delivered, one column a position, one row a scenario. Nothing here is computed: these are the inputs.
## Term (FX)
The exchange rate of every currency in every scenario, in US dollars per unit, with today's rate (Today (base), USD per unit) in the first row. Scenarios move exchange rates as well as prices, so currency risk is included.
## Term (PnL): the position columns, Portfolio, Rank, 10-day sum, V and E
The heart of the workbook. Each position column is its P&L in the reporting currency under every scenario (steps 1 and 2). Portfolio is their sum for each scenario (step 3). Rank orders the scenarios worst first (step 4). 10-day sum adds ten consecutive Portfolio values (step 9). V marks VaR's scenario and E the expected-shortfall tail. The helper columns at the right recompute the portfolio without one position, for incremental VaR.
## Term (Provenance)
Where every input came from (dataset, table version, file, business date), which settings were used, who made the workbook and when, and which engine version answered. Use it to reproduce or audit any figure.
